How we got here

greenhorn is a hypothesis with working code. This page tells how the design changed from a first idea to a hypothesis, what we got wrong on the way, and what we still do not know.

The problem

A reviewer who is new to a product opens a pull request description that is full of project words. They cannot follow it. We already had tools for the sentence: controlled-English rules and Hemingway rules. They fix how a sentence is written. They do not fix which concepts a stranger needs, in what order, or with which example. We wanted to close that gap.

Our first design

Our first idea was a Context block at the top of the pull request description. It held a list of up to 5 terms with one-sentence definitions. Next came a one-line before and after. Then came the brief. The author, or the model that writes for the author, picked the terms. The caps (5 terms, one line) were guesses.

We attacked it before building it

Before we built anything, we attacked our own design. We found seven problems.

  1. Definitions first and the example second contradicted the sources we cited. Those sources say concrete before abstract.
  2. A terms block at the top makes the reader flip between the block and the text.
  3. Authors cannot see their own jargon, so the author cannot be the one who finds the terms.
  4. A near analogy can mislead, so an analogy needs its limit stated.
  5. The caps were guesses.
  6. A neighbouring tool already existed. coldread by patrykwiener fixes text for a reader who was not in the session. greenhorn targets a different gap: the domain concepts that a newcomer lacks. We have not compared the two tools on the same documents.
  7. Most of our citations came from reviews and summaries, not from the papers.

The design changed in these ways:

  • A term gets its definition inline, at first use.
  • One concrete example comes before the mechanism.
  • A separate reader with no context finds the stumbles.
  • We take the answers from the code and never invent them.
  • An analogy appears only with its limit.
  • The output is skippable.
  • The limits stay as config defaults, not fixed numbers.

Then we read the papers

Problem 7 sent us to the papers themselves. We read 19 in full and 2 by abstract only. The table at the end lists each one.

Some of our ideas held up, with limits.

  • Rawson et al. (2015) ran three experiments with 131, 176 and 197 university students. Students who studied examples classified new examples better than students who only re-studied definitions (d = 0.74 to 1.67). The test used short concepts, not documents.
  • Sweller and Cooper (1985) ran four experiments with 20 to 40 Year 8 and Year 9 students learning algebra. Students who studied worked examples spent less time on practice. In Experiments 3 and 4 they also solved the test problems faster. The authors say the practical implications must be tested directly. The test compared studying solved problems with solving them. It did not compare examples with definitions.
  • Kalyuga et al. (2003) reviewed studies in which help for beginners lost its benefit, or hurt, more experienced learners. This is why our output is skippable. We know the studies only through the review, and none tests a skippable section.
  • Gray and Holyoak (2021) reviewed analogy research for classroom STEM teaching. They recommend that instructors "point out the limits of the analogy". That is a review's recommendation, not an experiment on that step.
  • Bacchelli and Bird (2013) studied code review at Microsoft. They found that understanding the change is the key challenge for reviewers. The study tests no fix.
  • Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber (1989) found that participants who knew a company's earnings could not ignore that knowledge when they predicted the estimates of people who did not. Pinker advises, in an essay and not in a study, that writers show a draft to people like the intended audience. These support why the author cannot be the one who finds the terms. They do not show that our fix works. The 1989 task was forecasting, not writing.
  • For inline definitions, the nearest test is Yeung, Jin and Sweller (1998, abstract only). They reported that definitions inside a passage helped 5th graders' comprehension but reduced vocabulary learning, and that adults showed the reverse. We found no test of inline definitions against a glossary for technical documents. Inline definitions stay a design choice.
  • Chandler and Sweller (1992) ran two experiments on material that points to other material. In the second, 20 educational psychology students read an experimental report. In one version the parts that refer to each other sat together. Those students spent less time and scored higher on test questions. The authors warn that experts or harder reports may give different results. The material was a report, not definitions or jargon.

Other ideas did not hold up. For each one, we believed something, the paper said something else, and we changed our claim.

  1. Order. We believed that an example before the definition beats a definition first. In the one direct test (Rawson et al.), performance was similar when examples came before or after the definition. So we dropped our claim about order. The tool still opens with an example, and we claim no benefit from that order.
  2. ICER 2023. We believed that Hassan et al. showed that novices learn better from concrete to abstract. The paper is a qualitative study of 42 interviews with undergraduates in an introductory Python course. It measured no learning gain, and its authors call their suggestions conjecture. So we no longer cite it for that.
  3. Program comprehension. We leaned on studies of how people read code. Pennington (1987) studied 80 and 40 professional programmers, so experts. von Mayrhauser and Vans (1995) report mixed and switching strategies, and say that no one model accounts for all behavior. So we dropped the claim that novices read bottom-up and experts read top-down.
  4. Advance organizers. We believed that organizers work best when short and more abstract. We saw that in nothing we read. Luiten et al. (1979, a conference paper) analyzed 135 studies and reported a small average effect on learning (mean .21). So we dropped the claim.
  5. The 20% to 40% figure. We wrote that reviewers ask the author 20% to 40% of the time. Bacchelli and Bird report that reviewers said in interviews that they often (from 20% to 40% of the times) talked to the author in person. That is an interview report from one company. So we now say so.
  6. Plain rewriting. We counted Bullock et al. (2019) as support for plain rewriting. They ran an online experiment with 650 participants. They measured how fluent a text felt, not comprehension. Jargon lowered felt fluency, and hover-over definitions did not change it. So we cite it only for that.
  7. Analogies. We wrote that an analogy can teach a wrong model. Gray and Holyoak say that inappropriate prior knowledge can produce misconceptions unless the instructor maps the correspondences and states the limits. So we changed our wording.

What we could not read

We could not open some papers. The publisher page showed a login or returned a 403 error, and we found no open copy. These are Ausubel (1960), Brooks (1983), Masson and Waldron (1994), McNamara et al. (1996) and the journal version of Luiten, Ames and Ackerson (1980). We know McNamara et al. only through the Kalyuga et al. review. We read the 1979 conference version of Luiten et al. instead. We did not attempt Newton (1990). We read Chandler and Sweller (1992), but not their 1991 paper. No claim in this account leans on any of them.

We used Claude to find and read the sources. We checked the key numbers and quotes a second time against the text of the papers.

Then we tested the tool

We tested the tool, not only the idea. An independent audit tried to break the checker. It found real bugs, and each one got a regression test.

  • Two --disable flags did not combine.
  • Prose such as "Before the change it broke. After a while it worked." counted as a before and after example.
  • An unclosed code fence counted as an example and hid the rest of the text.
  • The parser slowed down quadratically on long input.

One weak point remains, and we state it openly. The reader agent has the setting tools: [] so that it has no tools. The Claude Code docs say that an omitted tools field inherits every tool, and they say nothing about an empty list. In Claude Code 2.1.316 we saw that tools: [] leaves the agent only its hand-back tool. A later version may change that. So we also list every tool that we know of in disallowedTools, and a test keeps both lines.

Where it stands

We have a hypothesis, not a result. A newcomer follows technical text better when a reader with no context marks where they get lost, when every gap is filled from the real source and not guessed, and when the text opens with one concrete example before the mechanism.

No study tests that combination for technical documents or for software newcomers. The numbers in the defaults (5 terms, threshold 3, 25 words) are untested guesses. The reader in the pipeline is a model, not a human newcomer.

These results would show we are wrong:

  1. Fresh readers explain the greenhorn rewrite no better than they explain the original.
  2. The example-first order confuses more readers than it helps.
  3. The model reader lists stumbles that human newcomers do not hit, and misses stumbles that they do hit.

The human eval tests all three. It compares three versions of each document: the original, the original after polish only, and the full greenhorn rewrite. Fresh readers see one version each. They explain the text in two sentences. Two scorers who do not know the version score each answer from 0 to 2 against the author's intent. Nobody ran it yet, and the folder holds no results.

Read the full protocol. If you run it, send us the results. We will publish them even if the result is a tie.
