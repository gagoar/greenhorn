Explain it to someone who has never seen your system.

greenhorn is a Claude Code plugin that rewrites technical text for a reader who is new to your system.

Machines write more of the code now. The person who reviews, runs or inherits a change is often new to it, even on their own team. We have no study of this. It is what we see in our own work, and it is why we built greenhorn.

The reader did not write it

Reviewing a change was hard before this. Bacchelli and Bird (2013) observed and surveyed developers at Microsoft. They report that understanding the change is the key aspect of code review, and interviewees named it as their main challenge. Their study tests no fix.

When a tool writes the change, the person who posts the description may know little more than the reviewer. The text must then carry what the code cannot say: what this is, why it exists, and one real case.

Why we built it this way

We did not start from the papers. We started from a design, attacked it, and then read the papers. Some of what we believed did not survive. This is what did, with what each study did and what it cannot tell us. The full account lists every source. Each paper name below links to the paper. Two links go to the publisher page, where the full text may need access.

1. The author cannot find their own gaps

The study. Camerer, Loewenstein and Weber (1989) ran economic experiments. People who knew a company's real earnings guessed what less informed people would estimate. What they knew biased them, and they did not fully ignore it. Markets cut the bias by about half. Feedback alone did not appear to reduce it.

What we built. A cold reader that sees only the text and a profile of who the reader is. It never sees your repository. It lists where it stops.

What we do not know. The task was forecasting, not writing. It does not show that your text is harder to read.

2. Show a worked example

The study. In four experiments, Sweller and Cooper (1985) taught algebra to Year 8 and Year 9 students, 20 to 40 in each experiment. Students who studied worked examples spent less time in practice in three of the four experiments. In the fourth, the researchers matched the time. On later problems with the same structure, they were faster and made fewer errors, with one exception. In one experiment, the groups that did the different-structure problems first showed no difference on either kind. They had no advantage on problems with a different structure.

What we built. One concrete example with real values, before the mechanism.

What we do not know. The subject was algebra, in small groups, and the test compared examples with solving problems, not with definitions. The benefit may not carry over to new cases, so our eval tests new cases.

3. Examples and definitions together

The study. Rawson, Thomas and Jacoby (2015) enrolled 131, 176 and 197 undergraduates in three experiments. In the main analyses, students who studied examples classified examples better than students who only re-studied definitions. The effect sizes in the results run from 0.40 to 1.43. The abstract reports 0.74 to 1.67. The two orders, example first or definition first, did not differ significantly.

What we built. Every term gets a definition, and the text carries a real example. The example comes first as a design choice. We claim no benefit from that order. Our default of one example is also a choice, not a finding.

What we do not know. The concepts were short and the test came soon after study. The study did not use documents.

4. Put the word next to the thing it explains

The study. Chandler and Sweller (1992) ran two experiments. Novices learned from material in which text and diagrams, or parts that refer to each other, sat together. The groups with the integrated material scored higher. In the first experiment, 4 of 10 items differed individually, and the authors call the effects at best moderate. In the second experiment they also spent less time, 190 seconds against 216. The authors call that significant at t(18) = 1.85, which is weaker than the usual two-sided cutoff. One experiment tested 20 apprentices and the other tested 20 university students.

What we built. Each term gets its definition at its first use, not in a block at the top.

What we do not know. The material was instructions and one report, not jargon. The authors warn that experts, or harder reports, may give different results.

5. Jargon changes how reading feels

The study. Bullock and colleagues (2019) recruited 650 online readers, and about 640 were in the analysis. They read short science texts. Jargon lowered how fluent the text felt, 4.57 against 5.27 on a seven-point scale. Hover-over definitions had no significant effect on that feeling.

What we built. greenhorn warns when a text defines more than five terms. No study backs that number.

What we do not know. The study measured how the text felt, not whether people understood it. The definitions were hover text, not inline text.

6. Help for beginners can hurt experts

The study. Kalyuga, Ayres, Chandler and Sweller (2003) reviewed studies of techniques that help beginners. For more experienced learners, those techniques can lose their effect and can even have negative effects.

What we built. The output is easy to skip, and you choose a profile that says who the reader is.

What we do not know. We know those studies only through the review, and none of them tests a skippable section.

What nobody has tested

Our hypothesis: a newcomer follows technical text better when three things hold. A reader with no context marks where they get lost. Claude fills each gap from the real source. The text opens with one concrete example.

No study tests this combination on technical documents for software newcomers. The method is a hypothesis.

We wrote the human eval. It has no results yet. Read the protocol. If you run it, send us the result. We publish it even if the result is a tie.

We checked ourselves

On 2 November 2026 we asked five separate Claude agents to read every claim on our evidence page again, against the text of each paper. They found errors that our first check missed. The Sweller and Cooper first experiment had 62 students, not the 18 we wrote. Chandler and Sweller tested 20 of 26 apprentices, not 26. We wrote that people in the Camerer study could not ignore what they knew. The paper shows a partial bias.

We fixed all of it in public. The agents are models too. They quoted the paper text, so a person can check each finding. See the account.

What it touches

  • It reads the text that you give it. It reads your repository when it answers a stumble.
  • The reader agent has no tools and sees only the text. This relies on Claude Code behavior that the docs do not describe. We tested it in version 2.1.316, and a test keeps the settings.
  • /greenhorn:explain pre-approves writes only under .greenhorn/ in your project, and runs only its own two checker scripts, with any arguments after the .greenhorn/ path. /greenhorn:test writes nothing. These settings are a guard, not a sandbox.
  • The scripts are TypeScript with no dependency. The two checker scripts import only Node's file module and each other. They make no network call and read no environment variable.
  • MIT license. No account, no token and no telemetry in the scripts.

What it cannot do

The reader is a model. It is not a human newcomer. It finds likely stumbles. A clean result does not prove that a text is clear. The final test is a real person.

We have not tested the limits, such as 5 terms per text. They are defaults. The checker reads the shape of a text. It cannot tell whether a definition is true.

Install

/plugin marketplace add gagoar/gago-plugins
/plugin install greenhorn@gago-plugins

The install uses two commands. The helper plugins unmistakable and iceberg come with greenhorn. If one is missing, /greenhorn:explain stops and prints the command to install it. The guide lists every command.

The same author wrote foghorn, which writes technical release notes in the same spirit.
